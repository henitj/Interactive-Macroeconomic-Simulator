Economics in Plain English

GDP (Gross Domestic Product)

The total value of everything a country produces in a year. Think of it as the size of the national paycheck. When GDP grows, businesses usually hire more people. When it shrinks, people lose jobs.

Unemployment

The share of people looking for work who cannot find it. Low unemployment is good for families. High unemployment means less spending, which makes the whole economy weaker.

Inflation (CPI)

The rate at which prices increase over time. A small amount (around 2%) is healthy — it encourages people to spend rather than hoard cash. Too much inflation means your salary buys less food, gas, and rent.

Federal Funds Rate

The interest rate set by the central bank. It affects every loan in the country — credit cards, car loans, mortgages. When the rate rises, borrowing costs rise, which usually cools inflation but can slow hiring.

Money Supply Growth

How fast the total amount of money in the economy is growing. More money in circulation can boost growth, but too much too quickly can cause prices to jump.

Tax Rate

The percentage of income the government collects. Higher taxes mean more funding for public services but less take-home pay for workers. Lower taxes leave more money in pockets but reduce government budgets.

Supply Friction

A simple way to say "how hard it is to make and deliver things." When supply chains break, factories slow down, and prices rise — even if people still want to buy.

Market Sentiment

How optimistic or scared people feel about the future. When people feel confident, they invest, spend, and take risks. When fear spreads, people save money and avoid buying big items.
